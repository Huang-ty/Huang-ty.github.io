Label the original and new equilibria, write the exogenous shock beside the curve that shifts, and mark the final changes in Y and i. A change in an axis variable ordinarily moves along a fixed curve; a change in something held fixed when constructing that curve can shift it.

10.3 Common mistakes and their repairs

Mistake Repair
Adding sales of both intermediate and final goods Count final output or sum value added
Treating value added as profit Wages are part of value added
Treating a new house as household consumption New residential construction is investment
Counting a transfer as G Trace its effect through net taxes and household spending
Forgetting negative inventories when old output is sold Record the offsetting inventory withdrawal
Averaging two growth rates to form a Fisher link Take the square root of the product of the two growth factors
Using 3,050 as the tutorial’s chained year 1 level Divide 3,366 by F₂, giving 3,045.78
Interpreting disinflation as falling prices Prices still rise when inflation remains positive
Using total population in the unemployment denominator Use N + U
Using 1/(1 − c₁) for every model Check investment, taxes, and interest-rate assumptions
Giving a positive tax multiplier for ΔT The multiplier on a tax increase is negative
Saying T rises but public saving is unchanged At fixed G, T − G rises
Equating bond price with bond yield They move in opposite directions for a fixed payoff
Saying Y rising shifts LM At fixed M/P it causes movement along LM
Saying fiscal expansion always reduces total I Compare the sales and interest-rate effects
Treating nominal M and real M/P as identical Account for the price-level convention

10.4 Retrieval questions

Cover the answers below and explain each result in complete sentences before using formulas.

  1. Why do production, income, and expenditure measure the same GDP?
  2. Why is a firm’s wage bill not subtracted from value added?
  3. Can real GDP exceed nominal GDP? Give the relevant price comparison.
  4. How does changing a chain series’ reference year differ from changing fixed-base prices?
  5. Why can unemployment fall without employment rising?
  6. If c₁ = 0.8, what are the spending and tax multipliers in the basic Keynesian cross?
  7. With that MPC, what happens to Y if G rises by 20 and T rises by 20?
  8. Why does an economy-wide attempt to save more fail to increase saving in the fixed-investment model?
  9. A bond pays 100 next year and costs 96 today. What is its yield?
  10. If nominal income rises while money supply is fixed, what happens to i and bond prices?
  11. Why is IS downward-sloping, while the course’s LM is upward-sloping?
  12. A government raises T. What happens to C, public saving, private saving, and I in IS–LM?
  13. Why does monetary expansion unambiguously raise investment in the standard model?
  14. What policy mix can reduce the budget deficit while keeping output unchanged?
  15. Why is a zero-lower-bound limitation not the same assumption as an interest-rate target?

Answer key

1. Producing final output generates value added, that value added generates factor income, and the output is used as final expenditure or inventory investment. Consistent accounting links all three measures.

2. Value added is the income available to labour and capital after intermediate inputs are paid for. Subtracting wages would move toward profit instead.

3. Yes. For example, if the base-year prices used in real GDP exceed current prices, the constant-price value can exceed the nominal value. This is not a contradiction.

4. A reference-year change rescales a given chain series. A fixed-base price change alters the weights placed on different quantities and can change growth rates.

5. People can stop searching and leave both U and L. The unemployment rate can then fall while N is unchanged.

6. Spending multiplier = 1/0.2 = 5. Tax multiplier = −0.8/0.2 = −4.

7. ΔY = 5 × 20 − 4 × 20 = 20, under the basic model’s fixed-investment and lump-sum-tax assumptions.

8. Lower consumption reduces demand and equilibrium income. With I, T, and G fixed, equilibrium private saving is fixed by S = I − T + G.

9. i = 4/96 = 0.0416667, approximately 4.1667%.

10. Money demand rises. The equilibrium interest rate rises and the bond price falls.

11. A higher i reduces investment and goods-market equilibrium Y. A higher Y raises money demand, requiring a higher i at fixed real money supply.

12. C falls, public saving rises, and private saving falls. Investment is ambiguous because both Y and i fall.

13. It raises Y and lowers i, so both determinants stimulate investment.

14. Fiscal contraction with sufficient monetary expansion. The required easing lowers i enough to offset the reduction in goods demand, if the lower bound does not prevent it.

15. A rate target is a policy choice supported by an accommodating money supply. A liquidity trap reflects the inability to reduce the rate further through the model’s conventional money-for-bonds operation.
