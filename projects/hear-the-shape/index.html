THE QUESTION

Imagine a drum behind a curtain. A perfect instrument gives you every natural vibration frequency. You know the material and tension. Could you draw the drumhead’s outline?

We’ll explore an ideal, uniform, flat drumhead with its edge held fixed. Here, “hearing” means knowing the complete frequency list, including repetitions. We’ll use visual experiments to explore it.

A nodal line has zero displacement throughout the motion. Neighboring regions can move in opposite directions. A real strike usually excites several modes at once; the motion is their superposition. Changing the outline changes which patterns fit the boundary.

With the physical conditions fixed, each frequency corresponds to an eigenvalue, proportional to frequency squared. The complete eigenvalue list, including repeats, is the spectrum.

03

THE SPECTRUM ALREADY KNOWS SOMETHING

You can hear the area.

The full frequency list contains more than a lowest note. At high frequencies, the rate at which modes accumulate reveals the drumhead’s area. For smooth boundaries and ordinary polygons, the complete spectrum also determines the perimeter. These are exact facts about the ideal model, not estimates from an everyday recording. [4]

So the question is tempting: if the spectrum knows that much, might it know the whole outline? The study of these connections between geometry and eigenvalues is called spectral geometry.

04

THE SURPRISE

Different shapes.
The same spectrum.

Mark Kac popularized the question in his 1966 essay. In 1992, Gordon, Webb and Wolpert published different planar drumheads with the same complete spectrum. Even an infinite frequency list need not determine one outline. [1]

The pair below comes from a later account by Buser and colleagues. Each region is built from seven congruent scalene triangles. Their outlines cannot be matched by moving, rotating or reflecting one onto the other. [2]

ISOSPECTRAL

All eigenvalues agree, including how many independent modes share each value.

NON-ISOMETRIC

The shapes differ: no rigid motion makes the two planar domains coincide.

How the proof works: transplant a vibration pattern+

Take any mode on drum A. Call its seven function pieces f₀,…,f₆. To build a mode on B, align reflected copies of selected pieces and add or subtract their values. Choose a target triangle to inspect its rule. [2]

g₀ = f₁ + f₂ + f₄

Align pieces 1, 2 and 4 with target triangle 0, then add their values point by point.

Green + adds a function; rust − subtracts it. These colors identify terms, not displacement. Each symbol stands for a whole function after geometric alignment, not a number or a loose physical tile.

  1. Keep the same eigenvalue. Reflection preserves the Laplacian. Linearity lets aligned pieces with the same λ be combined.
  2. Make the pieces fit. The particular rules match across internal seams and make the sum vanish at the fixed outer edge. A minus sign represents a sign-reversed reflected function.
  3. Account for every mode. The invertible correspondence acts on each λ-eigenspace, preserving its dimension. This gives equal eigenvalues and multiplicities throughout the spectrum.

This is a guide to the published proof, not a simulated eigenmode or a proof from a few matching frequencies. Arbitrary triangle rearrangements do not satisfy these rules. Read Buser et al., §2 and Figure 3.

WHAT YOU CAN TAKE AWAY

You can hear the area.
You may still not know
the outline.

Frequency is not the whole recording. A recording also depends on which modes are excited, their amplitudes and how the vibration is measured.

The model matters. This result concerns ideal planar membranes under specified conditions. It is not a claim about every object or every possible measurement.

A QUESTION TO TAKE WITH YOU

What if you knew it was a triangle?

Within the class of triangular drumheads, the complete Dirichlet spectrum does determine the triangle up to congruence. Extra information about the possible shapes can restore uniqueness. [5] The next question is therefore: which geometric features, and which classes of shapes, can we recover?

GO ONE STEP DEEPERThe mathematics behind the motion+

A spatial mode φ is an eigenfunction of the negative Laplacian: −Δφ = λφ, with φ = 0 on the boundary. Here Δφ = ∂²φ/∂x² + ∂²φ/∂y² measures local spatial curvature. The wave equation utt = c²Δu relates it to acceleration. Substituting u = φ cos(2πft) gives the eigenvalue equation and the frequency relation below.

f = c√λ / 2π

For a rectangle of side lengths a and b, with positive integers m and n:

φmn(x,y) = sin(mπx/a) sin(nπy/b)
λmn = π²(m²/a² + n²/b²)

Our animated displacement is Aφmn cos(2πfmnt). The wave speed c stays fixed. Doubling a and b divides λ by four and f by two. A single common slowdown is applied to every animated frequency.

For a fixed wave speed c, the eigenvalue list and the frequency list determine each other. “Spectrum” counts repeated eigenvalues with their multiplicities. For example, on a square, the (1, 2) and (2, 1) patterns are independent modes with the same eigenvalue.

The area experiment

For a rectangle, λmn = π²(m²/a² + n²/b²), m,n ≥ 1. Let N(Λ) count all pairs with λmn ≤ Λ, including repeated values. For the positive operator −Δ, Weyl’s law is N(Λ)/Λ → A/(4π). Thus 4πN(Λ)/Λ approaches area A. Counting modes up to a frequency F is equivalent to using Λ = (2πF/c)².

For a bounded smooth planar domain, the heat trace has the first two terms Σe−tλ = A/(4πt) − L/(8√(πt)) + O(1) as t → 0, where L is perimeter. Polygons have the same first two terms, with corner contributions at the constant term. This explains why the complete spectrum fixes both A and L. Kac uses a different operator normalization; the constants here match our −Δ convention. [4]
