Can One Hear the Shape of a Drum?

Imagine knowing every natural frequency of a drumhead. Would that be enough to draw its outline?

You hear a drum behind a curtain. Could you tell whether its surface is round, square, or some stranger shape?

Recognizing a real instrument involves many clues: its material, how it is struck, and the room around it. Let us ask a more precise question. Imagine an ideal, uniform drumhead with its edge held fixed. Suppose a perfect measuring device gives you every natural vibration frequency. If the material and tension are known, does that list uniquely reveal the shape?

The surprising answer is: not always.

A drumhead has more than one way to vibrate

Think first of a vibrating string. Its simplest standing-wave pattern moves together between two fixed ends. Other patterns have stationary points inside the string, dividing it into sections that move in opposite directions.

A drumhead has similar patterns spread across a surface. These are called vibration modes. Each mode has a natural frequency. Its stationary curves are called nodal lines.

The diagram below shows three modes of one rectangular drumhead. The plus and minus signs indicate opposite displacements in a snapshot. They reverse as the membrane oscillates; the dashed nodal lines remain still.

Try a prediction

Suppose we double both side lengths, keeping the tension and surface density unchanged. Will the frequencies double, halve, or stay the same?

They halve. In the mathematical formula, each side length appears squared in a denominator; taking the square root converts that change into a factor of one half.

So geometry affects vibration. It is tempting to reverse the argument: perhaps sufficiently detailed vibration information must reveal the geometry.

But a forward relationship need not have a unique inverse.

Different shapes can share a spectrum

Mathematicians call the full list of eigenvalues the spectrum. For our membrane model, each eigenvalue determines a natural frequency. Repeated eigenvalues are counted with their multiplicities.

In 1992, Carolyn Gordon, David Webb and Scott Wolpert published planar domains that were isospectral but non-isometric: their entire spectra agreed, although their shapes differed. This gave a negative answer to the famous drum question. Their paper establishes the counterexample.

Figure 2. A reconstructed example from Buser and colleagues’ later account. Only the outer boundary is fixed; the internal lines show its construction.

Here, isospectral means agreement throughout the infinite eigenvalue list, not just a few similar low frequencies. Non-isometric means the two planar domains cannot be made to coincide by a rigid motion, including reflection.

How could this happen?

For the illustrated pair, a proof technique called transplantation relates the vibration modes. Pieces of an eigenfunction are reflected and combined on the other domain. The construction makes them fit together and satisfy the fixed-edge condition while preserving the eigenvalue. The correspondence also preserves multiplicities.

This is a mathematical argument, rather than evidence from two recordings or a short frequency table. Buser and colleagues explain the construction and transplantation.

What the result does—and does not—say

The conclusion is that a complete spectrum need not identify one unique shape. It does not say that any two shapes share a spectrum, or that vibration measurements contain no geometric information.

Nor does equal frequency information automatically make ordinary recordings identical. A recording also depends on which modes are excited, their amplitudes, and how the vibration is measured.

The curtain therefore hides a deeper problem than recognizing an instrument. Even an ideal frequency measurement can leave more than one possible shape behind it. Mathematics lets us identify that ambiguity precisely.

Optional mathematical box

A mode shape φ satisfies −Δφ = λφ, with φ = 0 on the boundary. The Laplacian Δ measures spatial variation. The eigenfunction φ gives the vibration pattern, while its eigenvalue λ determines the frequency: f = c√λ/(2π), where c is the membrane wave speed.
